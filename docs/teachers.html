For Teachers

What this site is

Hox Insect Lab is a free teaching site about how genes shape an insect's body. Students first work through short Learn lessons. Then they experiment in the Lab, a 3D model where they switch Hox genes on or off and see the predicted body plan.

The site is made for ages 10 to 14 and works for older students too. The Lab has a Scientist mode that adds evidence labels, gene records and more data.

Learning goals

After the lessons and the Lab, students can say:

  • I can explain that an insect body is built from repeating segments.
  • I can describe a Hox gene as a gene that tells a segment which body part to build.
  • I can predict what changes in a body plan when one Hox gene is switched off or on in the wrong place.
  • I can compare the same change in two insects, such as a fly and a beetle.
  • I can tell the difference between a result seen in a real experiment and a guess made by the model.
  • I can name one thing a simple model gets right and one thing it leaves out.

Standards

This activity connects to the standards below. It is not a complete way to meet any of them, and it does not replace your own assessment.

  • NGSS MS-LS3-1 (mutations and effects on an organism). Students use a model to see how losing or misplacing a gene's activity changes an organism's structure. The Lab models gene on and off, not single DNA changes.
  • NGSS HS-LS1-1 (DNA and the proteins that do a cell's work). Hox genes make proteins that switch other genes on, so students see how information in DNA leads to body structure. The link is indirect, because the Lab shows the outcome and not the protein.
  • NGSS HS-LS3-1 (asking questions about genes and traits). Students ask "what if" questions, test them in the Lab, and check them against published experiments listed on the About page.

Worksheet

Students use the Learn page and the Lab. Use the fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) unless a question says otherwise. Turn on Scientist mode for question 8. Reset the Lab between questions.

  1. How many Hox genes can you switch in the Lab? Write their names in order.
  2. Leave the fly unedited. Which segment is the third thorax segment (T3), and what body parts does it carry?
  3. Switch off the gene Ubx in T3 only (or pick "Four wings" from Fun mutants). What does the T3 segment turn into? What happens to the little balancing organs (halteres)? How many wings does the fly have now?
  4. Switch Antp on in the antenna segment (An) only. What does the antenna turn into?
  5. Switch off Antp in the whole fly. What does the middle leg (T2) turn into?
  6. Switch off Scr in the first thorax segment (T1) only. What happens to the foreleg? Does anything new appear on that segment?
  7. Choose the beetle Tribolium castaneum and switch off Ubx in T3 only. What does the hindwing become? Is it the same as in the fly? Why might the two animals look different even though the gene rule is the same?
  8. In the fly, switch off pb. Two segments change. Which change is labelled as seen in a real experiment, and which is labelled as the model's guess?
Answer key
  1. Eight: lab, pb, Dfd, Scr, Antp, Ubx, abdA, AbdB.
  2. T3 carries a hindleg and a haltere (a small balancing organ). With no edits the Lab reports "no identity change".
  3. T3 becomes a copy of T2. The hindleg becomes a midleg and the haltere becomes a wing, so the fly has four wings. The label says documented (Lewis, 1978). If students switch Ubx off in the whole fly instead, the model also changes A1, so they may count extra legs and wings there.
  4. The antenna turns into a midleg (An: antenna to midleg). Documented.
  5. The midleg turns into an antenna (T2: midleg to antenna). Documented.
  6. The foreleg becomes a midleg, and a wing appears on T1 (foreleg to midleg; no wing to wing). Documented (Struhl, 1982). If Scr is switched off in the whole fly, the labium (Lb) changes too, so use T1 only.
  7. The hindwing becomes an elytron (a hard wing case). The hindleg becomes a midleg, as in the fly. The rule is the same: T3 acts like T2. The beetle's T2 wing is an elytron, so a "T2 copy" looks different from a fly's wing.
  8. Labium (Lb): the labellum turns into a foreleg. This is documented in real experiments. Maxilla (Mx): the maxillary palp turns into a vestigial lobe. This is labelled "model extrapolation", meaning a model guess with no matching experiment.

Check each answer against the Lab with the settings in the question. The Lab text may word a body part slightly differently (for example "midleg" and "T2").

Common misconceptions

What students often thinkThe better idea
Hox genes make the legs.Hox genes tell a segment which body part to build. Other genes do the building. A Hox gene is more like a label on a segment than a leg factory.
A mutant insect like the four-winged fly could live in the wild.These animals are made in laboratories. The extra wings do not work as the real flight organs do, and the haltere that is lost helps a fly balance. Such an animal would struggle to survive.
One gene makes one body part.Hox genes work as a team. The part a segment builds depends on which genes are on there, and one gene can affect many segments.
Switching off a gene makes the body part vanish.Often the part turns into a different part (for example, a haltere into a wing). Sometimes a part is lost. The Lab shows both.
Hox genes are special to insects.Most animals have Hox genes, including humans. Insects are a good place to study them because the segments are easy to see.

What the model simplifies

Share these limits with students. They are part of good science.

  • The Lab is a rule-based model. It is not a true predictor of how a real animal would look.
  • It uses "posterior prevalence": the gene further toward the back usually wins when two genes are on in a segment. Some listed exceptions are built in, but real animals have more of them.
  • Body plans come from order-level templates (for example, one template for beetles). Individual species are not drawn separately.
  • Results with no matching published experiment are labelled as model extrapolation. Also, for some of the 44 experiment records on the About page, the rules give a different answer than the real animal did.
  • It shows the adult body plan only. Eggs, larvae and growth are not modelled.

Tips for the classroom

  • It works on Chromebooks, tablets and phones. A recent browser is all you need.
  • There are no accounts or sign-in, and nothing to install.
  • Progress (lesson stars and settings) is saved only in the student's own browser. A shared computer shows the previous user's stars until the browser data is cleared.
