    How the model works

    Insects share a set of eight Hox genes, usually clustered on one chromosome in the order they act along the body: lab, pb, Dfd, Scr, Antp, Ubx, abd-A, Abd-B. In some insects, including Drosophila, the cluster is split in two. Each segment's identity comes from the most posterior Hox gene switched on in it (posterior prevalence), with a few combinatorial rules: pb with Scr makes a labium, pb without Scr makes a maxillary palp (even over Antp), and Scr outranks Antp in the first thoracic segment.

    In this model, a segment with no Hox gene falls back to a ground state: an antenna-like ventral appendage (as in Antp loss, where legs become antennae) and, on the thorax or abdomen, a wing. The wing part is an extrapolation from the observation that the forewing needs no Hox input. Gnathal head segments with no Hox gene lose their mouthparts.

    Order-level body plans set the style of each part, so the same identity change gives a haltere-to-wing switch in a fly and a hindwing-to-elytron switch in a beetle. The model ignores dose, timing, parasegment boundaries, cofactors and downstream targets, and Hox genes switching each other on or off, so treat extrapolated results as hypotheses. Some real results differ by species: in Tribolium, losing Scr also removes pb from the labium, and losing abd-A leaves small abdominal appendages, while this model applies one rule to every insect. Changes matched to a published experiment are marked Documented.

    Not modelled: zen, bcd and ftz (Hox-derived genes without homeotic roles in insects), larval forms, caste and sex differences, and traits outside Hox control such as secondary winglessness.
